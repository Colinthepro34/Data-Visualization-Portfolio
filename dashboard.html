"So before I show you any charts, let me just put the scale of this in front of you."

This dashboard covers 4,000 students, enrolled across 17 different courses. Of those students, 49.93% eventually graduate — but 32.12% drop out before finishing. That's not a rounding error, that's a real chunk of students we're losing every year. I'm not showing a chart yet on purpose: at this point you don't have any context to compare a breakdown against, so a chart here would actually be premature. You just need the headline facts first.

The point of this page isn't any one number — it's the framing. Here's a student population, spread across 17 courses, and by the end of their studies, nearly a third of them never make it to graduation. That gap between the graduate rate and the dropout rate is really this entire presentation in one sentence: the rest of what follows is just one question — why, and can we see it coming?

So dropout isn't rare — it's a meaningful share of this population. The natural next question is: is it spread evenly across every course, or is it concentrated somewhere specific? Let's look at the overview.

The majority of students actually do graduate — just over half, at 50.95% — but the dropout slice is still substantial at 30.8%, with the rest still enrolled. That's the average across everyone though, and averages hide a lot. The bar chart next to it breaks the same students down by course, and that's where it gets interesting: bigger programs like Nursing carry far more volume than the rest, and when you look at the dropout-coloured share within each bar, it isn't proportional everywhere — a handful of programs carry a visibly heavier dropout share than the institution average. So dropout isn't evenly spread across the school. It's concentrated.

Worth flagging honestly: with only three categories, a donut is the more digestible choice for a general audience, but a bar chart is always the more rigorous way to compare three values precisely — it's a one-click swap since it's the same underlying fields, I just judged the donut friendlier here.

So we know outcomes cluster around specific courses. But a final label like "Dropout" or "Graduate" is the end of a story — it doesn't tell us how a student got there. To understand that, we need to follow individual students semester by semester. That's the Student Journey page.

S03 — Student Journey

The warning shows up in Semester 1

Instead of the final label, this page traces each student's path — Semester 1 result, into Semester 2 result, into final outcome — as a Sankey flow.

44.27%
Dropout rate (Basic Education, filtered)

"This is the same students as before, but now I'm not showing you where they ended up — I'm showing you how they got there."

Each band here is a Semester 1 performance category, flowing into a Semester 2 category, flowing into the final outcome. And watch the thickest flow out of "none passed" or "no units enrolled" in Semester 1 — students who struggled that early overwhelmingly end up in the dropout column by the end. That's a genuinely early signal. This isn't something we only discover in the final semester; it's visible in the very first one.

I used an imported Sankey visual here rather than something native to Power BI, and that's worth saying out loud before anyone asks: Power BI has no built-in flow chart, so importing one from AppSource is the standard route for this kind of question. Two separate stacked bars for Semester 1 and Semester 2 would show the category counts fine, but they'd lose the actual connective thread — that most of the students who passed nothing in Semester 1 also passed nothing in Semester 2, and that most of those became dropouts. That link only a flow diagram preserves.

So categorically, we can see the paths diverge early. But "passed some" versus "passed all" is a coarse bucket — I want to actually put numbers on that divergence: how much do grades and pass rates differ between these groups, and does that gap already exist in Semester 1, or does it open up later? That's the next page.

"Now I want to put real numbers behind that early-warning idea."

This chart tracks three groups — eventual Dropouts, Graduates, and students still Enrolled — on two things at once: how many of their units they're actually passing, and their average grade. Watch what happens moving from Semester 1 to Semester 2: the dropout group already sits noticeably lower on both axes early on, and by Semester 2 that gap has only widened — Graduates cluster near-total pass rates around 95% with the highest grades near 13.2, while Dropouts sit down around 33% passed and a 12.1 average. The size of each bubble is how many students are in that group, and its position is your risk signal, visually, before I say a single number.

I animated this rather than showing two static charts side by side because with only two time points, motion lets you watch the gap open up instead of forcing you to mentally diff two separate images. It's a deliberate, controlled use of animation, not a gimmick — and I'll admit its one real limitation up front: a motion chart is harder to read with precision than a table, which is exactly why the "Selected Semester" card sits next to it, so you always know which frame you're looking at.

If that gap is already visible in Semester 1 — before anyone's officially labelled a "dropout" — then the obvious next question is a practical one: can we actually turn that into an early-warning system? Can we flag at-risk students while there's still time to help them, instead of finding out at the end? That's what the last page does.

"So here's the practical version of everything we've just seen."

This slider lets me set a rule: flag any student who passed fewer than a set number of units in Semester 1 as "at risk." Right now, at a threshold of 2 units, that flags 734 students, catches 45.72% of the real dropouts, and is right 87.68% of the time it flags someone. Watch what happens as I move the slider — as I raise the threshold, I catch more of the real dropouts, that's "Dropouts Caught" going up, but I also start flagging more students who were actually fine, so precision drops. There's no free lunch here; it's a genuine trade-off, and that's exactly the kind of decision a student-success office would have to make.

The "right" cutoff isn't a fixed fact, it's a policy choice — so instead of hardcoding one number, I built it as an adjustable parameter so you can see, live, what happens to the rule's accuracy as that line moves. And this isn't just an abstract percentage: the table below shows exactly which students would get flagged today, and the course chart shows which programs need the most attention right now.

So that's the arc of this dashboard — from noticing the problem, to seeing where it concentrates, to watching it develop, to actually doing something about it. Let me pull that together.

Closing

Could we have seen it coming?

So here's what this dashboard adds up to. Dropout isn't random, and it isn't a Semester-4 surprise — it's visible, in both the categories students pass through and the actual numbers behind their grades, as early as Semester 1. That's not just an academic finding, it's an actionable one, which is why the story doesn't stop at charts: the last page turns that signal into a rule you can actually tune, with an honest look at its trade-offs, rather than a black-box flag.

If this project went further, the obvious next step would be replacing that simple units-passed threshold with a proper trained model — logistic regression or similar. But the value of building it this way first is that every stakeholder can see, transparently, exactly why someone got flagged, before ever handing that decision to a black box.
